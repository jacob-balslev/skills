# Positioning Source Notes

> Skill: `positioning`
> Retrieved: 2026-05-29
> Purpose: local grounding notes for April Dunford's product-positioning methodology.

## Primary Sources

- April Dunford, "A Quickstart Guide to Positioning"
  - URL: https://www.aprildunford.com/post/a-quickstart-guide-to-positioning
  - Evidence used: Dunford describes positioning as a methodology with five interdependent components: competitive alternatives, differentiated features or capabilities, value for customers, target customer segmentation, and market category.
  - Evidence used: The workflow starts from competitive alternatives, then asks what the product has that alternatives do not, what customer value those attributes enable, which customers care most, and which market category makes that value obvious.
  - Failure modes used in the skill: defining competitors from the company's perspective rather than the customer's actual alternative; creating a new category when an existing category could make the value clearer.

- April Dunford, "Obviously Awesome - How to Nail Product Positioning so Customers Get it, Buy it, Love it"
  - URL: https://www.aprildunford.com/books
  - Evidence used: Dunford's official book page describes Obviously Awesome as a step-by-step positioning process for B2B technology companies and links the method to finding a product's "secret sauce" and the buyers who care.
  - Evidence used: The page offers a workbook with a positioning canvas and sales-pitch storyboard, supporting the skill's emphasis on capturing the position so marketing, sales, product, and customer success can reuse it.

## Supplemental Source for the 10-Step Sequence

- Summaries.com, "Obviously Awesome" one-page summary
  - URL: https://public.summaries.com/files/1-page-summary/obviously-awesome.pdf
  - Evidence used: Supplemental public summary of the 10-step sequence from the book: understand happiest customers, form the team, discard positioning baggage, list true competitive alternatives, isolate unique attributes, map attributes to value themes, identify who cares most, choose the market frame, optionally layer on a trend, and capture/share the positioning.
  - Evidence caveat: This is not April Dunford's official site. The skill treats Dunford's official book and quickstart pages as the primary sources, and uses this summary only to cross-check the public naming of the book's 10-step sequence.

## Stable Framework Facts Used by the Skill

- Positioning is context-setting for the product, not a tagline, messaging copy, brand identity, or a standalone campaign.
- The method is relative: the value only becomes obvious when compared against what customers would actually do without the product.
- Unique attributes are not inherently valuable. They matter only when they enable value that a target customer segment cares about.
- Market category is a strategic frame of reference. It creates buyer assumptions about alternatives, pricing, features, risk, and the buying process.
- A trend is optional. It should create urgency or "why now" only when it reinforces the chosen market context and value.
- The output must be operationalized, not left as a fill-in-the-blank statement. The final position should guide sales narrative, website messaging, product roadmap focus, customer-success handoffs, and proof collection.

## Failure Modes to Preserve

- Starting with an internal target segment instead of real competitive alternatives.
- Listing phantom competitors that buyers do not consider.
- Treating "do nothing" or status quo as non-competition.
- Claiming differentiation without mapping attributes to customer value.
- Picking a broad market category that hides the product's real advantage.
- Creating a new category because it sounds ambitious, not because no existing frame makes the value obvious.
- Layering on a trend before market category and value are clear.
- Writing positioning as a slogan instead of a reusable source of truth for go-to-market teams.
